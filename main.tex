\title [mode = title]{A reproducible benchmark for transformation uncertainty in aquifer-test parameter inference}

\author[1]{Ying-Fan Lin}
\ead{yflin1110@cycu.edu.tw}

\address[1]{Department of Civil Engineering, Chung Yuan Christian University, Taoyuan, Taiwan}

\begin{abstract}
Aquifer-test transmissivity and storage are not direct observations. They are inferred by fitting a response model to drawdown. This paper introduces a reproducible computational benchmark that quantifies how that drawdown-to-parameter transformation affects inferred aquifer properties before they enter groundwater models, pumping-capacity calculations, or response-time estimates. Four analytical interpretations--Theis confined flow, Hantush--Jacob leakage, lagging Darcy without leakage, and lagging Darcy with leakage--are fitted to controlled numerical responses and two field pumping tests. A 10,000 scenario groundwater-flow ensemble generated 160,012 fitted interpretation records across homogeneous, leaky, finite-boundary, heterogeneous, and combined settings. The benchmark links support-matched reference parameters, analytical limiting checks, grid sensitivity checks, and scenario-conditioned model factors. Analytical verification confirmed physical consistency in 22 of 25 limiting cases, and numerical responses aligned with analytical references in 30 of 32 configurations. Benchmark-transfer regressions predicted the 90th percentile absolute log model factor with cross-validated $R^2$ values of 0.48, 0.51, and 0.55 for transmissivity, storage, and response time. Application to the Massachusetts fractured-bedrock and Lovelock Valley basin-fill cases produced benchmark-conditioned model-factor COV values of 0.61--0.90 and 0.31--0.51, respectively. Model-averaged decision diagnostics showed that the selected interpretation can understate hydraulic capacity and response-time spread. The workflow evaluates lagging Darcy as one candidate interpretation and reports transformation uncertainty through response residuals, benchmark support, field applicability, and inherited decision consequences.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Groundwater models, capture calculations, dewatering designs, geothermal screening, and water-management assessments commonly inherit transmissivity and storage from pumping-test reports \citep{freeze_cherry1979,bear1979,anderson2015}. Those quantities are not measured directly. They are inferred by transforming observed drawdown through an interpretation model, such as Theis confined flow, Hantush--Jacob leakage, delayed-yield, wellbore-storage, or generalized radial-flow solutions \citep{theis1935,cooper_jacob1946,hantush_jacob1955,boulton1954,papadopulos_cooper1967,neuman1975,barker1988}. This front-end transformation can introduce model-form error before stochastic groundwater modeling, Bayesian model averaging, hydraulic tomography, or management optimization begins \citep{rojas2008,tsai2010,tsai_elshall2013,doherty2015}.

Mature groundwater uncertainty methods usually operate after a model ensemble, parameterization, or prior structure has already been specified. Slug-test support studies, pumping-test upscaling, aquifer-scale inference, and multimodel groundwater prediction address related downstream questions \citep{beckie_harvey2002,manewell2022,manewell2024,naderi_gupta2023,li_tsai2009}. Pumping-test interpretation begins one step earlier, when a drawdown record is converted into apparent transmissivity, storage, leakage, or response-time parameters. If the response model omits temporal memory, leakage, boundary behavior, or unresolved support effects, the mismatch can be hidden inside fitted aquifer parameters and then mistaken for hydrogeologic heterogeneity.

This paper develops a reproducible benchmark for that upstream transformation step. Four analytical interpretation pathways form controlled drawdown-to-parameter mappings: Theis confined, Hantush--Jacob leaky, lagging Darcy without leakage, and lagging Darcy with leakage. Lagging Darcy enters the analysis as one diagnostic interpretation for temporal response structure within the conventional aquifer-test solution set. The computational contribution is a 10,000 scenario MODFLOW 6 benchmark containing controlled transient responses, support-matched reference parameters, analytical limiting checks, grid-sensitivity checks, and scenario-conditioned model factors. The benchmark is then linked to two public field data sets, a Massachusetts fractured-bedrock test and the Lovelock Valley MWP2 basin-fill test, through response diagnostics and model-averaged decision quantities \citep{cho2004pumping,cho2008characterization,nadler2020report,nadler2020data}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Transformation uncertainty framework}

The analysis treats aquifer-test interpretation as a transformation model. The measured input is a drawdown response $s_{\mathrm{obs}}(t,r)$, and the derived outputs are apparent aquifer parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}_M$, including transmissivity $T$, storage $S$, leakage factor $B$, or lagging response times under interpretation model $M$. For a fitted response $s_M(t,r;\boldsymbol{\theta}_M)$, the response-level log model factor is

\begin{equation}
\eta_{M,i,j}=\log[s_{\mathrm{obs},i}(t_j)+c]-\log[s_{M,i}(t_j;\boldsymbol{\theta}_M)+c],
\label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $i$ denotes an observation well, $j$ denotes time, and $c$ is a small positive offset used only to keep the log transformation defined. The dispersion $SD_\epsilon$ of $\eta$ quantifies response-level transformation residual structure. For numerical benchmark cases with known support-matched reference values, parameter model factors are also computed, for example $M_T=T_{\mathrm{app}}/T_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $M_S=S_{\mathrm{app}}/S_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Lognormal coefficient of variation values are computed from the standard deviation of $\log M$.

\subsection{Field data and analytical interpretations}

The field component uses two public pumping-test data sets with contrasting hydrogeologic settings and observation geometries. The Massachusetts case is a fractured crystalline bedrock pumping test with 12 retained observation wells and 420 drawdown observations \citep{cho2004pumping,cho2008characterization}. The retained diagnostic subset is analyzed as a constant-rate response with $Q=\SI{1.2e-4}{m^3.s^{-1}}$, while the original variable-rate history remains an applicability condition. The Lovelock Valley case is USGS multiwell pumping test 2 in basin-fill sediments of the lower Humboldt River Basin, with pumping and recovery represented through superposition \citep{nadler2020report,nadler2020data}.

Both field cases are fitted using four analytical interpretations. Theis confined flow is the classical no-leakage reference. Hantush--Jacob is the conventional leaky-aquifer reference. Lagging Darcy without leakage introduces flux and storage-release lag times to diagnose temporal memory, and lagging Darcy with leakage combines that temporal response with leakage. The lagging solutions are evaluated in the Laplace domain and inverted using the Stehfest algorithm \citep{stehfest1970}. Fits minimize log-response residuals using the same well coordinates, observation records, and pumping representation for all interpretations. AIC, BIC, local parameter correlation, boundary-hit checks, and time-window residual summaries are retained as complexity and identifiability diagnostics.

\subsection{Numerical groundwater-flow benchmark}

The controlled benchmark uses MODFLOW 6 as a reproducible groundwater-flow response generator \citep{langevin2017,bakker2016}. In this role, MODFLOW 6 supplies transient drawdown responses for testing how analytical interpretations recover apparent aquifer parameters. The ensemble contains 10,000 scenarios in ten classes: homogeneous confined flow, distributed leakage, finite no-flow boundary, constant-head boundary, heterogeneous hydraulic conductivity, anisotropic heterogeneous conductivity, heterogeneous storage, heterogeneous leakage, heterogeneous boundary, and combined leakage and boundary settings. Each class contributes 1000 scenarios.

The design spans grid spacings of 5.0--10.0 m, square domains of 405--1205 m, six observation layouts with 3--5 wells, logarithmically spaced records over 1--10 days, and water-level noise from 0 to 0.01 m. Heterogeneous cases use correlated lognormal hydraulic conductivity or storage fields. A scenario-specific effective pumping support radius defines $T_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{ref}}$ before fitting. The support radius combines hydraulic diffusivity, pumping duration, maximum observation distance, leakage length where present, and boundary or domain limits. This prevents a numerical cell value from being treated as the same object as a pumping-test parameter.

Each numerical response is refitted with the same four analytical interpretations. The resulting records are screened by removing active boundary-flag fits and retaining interpretations within $\Delta BIC \leq 10$ of the best interpretation for the same response. Analytical limit checks test whether the implemented formulas recover known limiting behavior. Numerical consistency checks compare MODFLOW-generated homogeneous and leaky responses against analytical reference responses across grid spacings.

\subsection{Benchmark transfer and decision diagnostic}

The numerical benchmark contains controlled reference values, and the field cases contain real response diagnostics. Benchmark-transfer regressions estimate the magnitude of parameter model factors that a field test may inherit when its diagnostics resemble parts of the numerical ensemble. Predictors are restricted to quantities available in both numerical and field tests: response residual dispersion, observation distance, BIC support, boundary and leakage flags, parameter spread, and fitted pathway class. Regression targets are the 90th percentile absolute log model factors for $T$, $S$, and response time within each numerical scenario.

The management diagnostic follows the Bayesian model averaging logic used in groundwater uncertainty analysis \citep{hoeting1999,li_tsai2009,tsai_elshall2013}. The four aquifer-test interpretations are treated as candidate models. Their fitted $T$ and $S$ values are propagated into hydraulic capacity, proportional to $T$, and characteristic response time, proportional to $S/T$. Strict BIC weights and a variance-window weighting scheme are reported. This diagnostic asks whether a later groundwater calculation can inherit overconfident inputs if aquifer-test interpretation uncertainty is collapsed into one fitted parameter set.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Benchmark verification and model factors}

The numerical benchmark generated 160,012 fitted interpretation and well records, of which 88,106 satisfied the screening criteria (Table~\ref{tab:mf6benchmark}). Analytical verification confirmed physical consistency in 22 of 25 limiting cases, and numerical responses aligned with analytical reference responses in 30 of 32 grid configurations (Figure~\ref{fig:mf6verification}). The remaining warnings identify near-well finite leakage and coarse-grid settings that require caution before field transfer. Those warnings delimit the usable benchmark domain.

The screened benchmark separates response fit from parameter recovery. Across class and interpretation groups, the median model-factor COV values are 2.06 for transmissivity, 1.27 for storage, and 1.21 for response time (Figure~\ref{fig:mf6cov}). Leaky, heterogeneous leakage, and combined boundary classes have wider upper tails. Variance attribution shows that response residual standard deviation is driven mainly by water-level noise and observation support, whereas transmissivity and storage model factors remain strongly interpretation dependent. A small drawdown residual does not guarantee that the inferred $T$ or $S$ is close to its support-matched reference value.

\subsection{Benchmark-conditioned transfer to field diagnostics}

Benchmark-transfer regressions predicted the 90th percentile absolute log model factor with cross-validated $R^2$ values of 0.48 for transmissivity, 0.51 for storage, and 0.55 for response time (Figure~\ref{fig:benchmarktransfer}). These moderate values establish a reproducible bridge from response diagnostics to conditional parameter uncertainty. Applied to the field cases, the p90-equivalent benchmark-transferred model-factor COV values are 0.61--0.90 for Massachusetts and 0.31--0.51 for Lovelock Valley. The values act as sensitivity brackets conditioned on the represented benchmark classes and field diagnostics.

\subsection{Field diagnostic evidence}

The two field cases place the benchmark in real aquifer-test settings with different geological supports (Figure~\ref{fig:layout}). Massachusetts is a small fractured-bedrock network, whereas Lovelock Valley is a basin-fill system with pumping and recovery observations. The four analytical interpretations were fitted consistently across both cases (Figures~\ref{fig:drawdown} and~\ref{fig:lovelockresponse}). The visual fits and residual summaries show that the lagging interpretations reduce structured response mismatch in both settings, while leakage support differs by case and well.

In Massachusetts, pooled log model factor dispersion decreases from 0.166 for Theis confined to 0.062 for lagging Darcy without leakage and 0.035 for lagging Darcy with leakage (Figure~\ref{fig:variancecov}). Hantush--Jacob and lagging leaky forms receive leakage support in selected wells, indicating that leakage or boundary-equivalent behavior should remain an explicit competing interpretation. In Lovelock Valley, pooled dispersion decreases from 0.246 for Theis confined to approximately 0.174 for the two lagging interpretations, while BIC support concentrates in the no-leakage lagging form (Figure~\ref{fig:lovelocksummary}). The site contrast matches the intended use of the four pathways: conventional leakage, temporal memory, and combined behavior are tested rather than assumed.

\subsection{Scope of inference and management consequence}

The synthesis figure separates the inference scope of the two field cases (Figure~\ref{fig:fieldexternal}). Both cases contain response-level diagnostics and pathway-dependent parameter spread. The Massachusetts case includes a within-network spatial-transfer diagnostic by excluding one observation well at a time; Lovelock adds an independent field consistency check under a different support scale. Neither field case by itself recovers true support-scale $T$, $S$, leakage, or lagging times. The numerical benchmark supplies conditional model factors, and the field applicability criteria define where those factors can be used as sensitivity brackets or conservative priors (Figure~\ref{fig:mf6fieldapp}).

The decision diagnostic shows why this upstream uncertainty matters for later groundwater calculations. Under variance-window BMA, the median hydraulic-capacity factor is 1.60 in Massachusetts and 1.18 in Lovelock Valley; the corresponding 95th percentile factors are 2.73 and 4.52 (Figure~\ref{fig:decisionconsequence}). Median response-time factors are 1.85 and 4.05, with 95th percentile values of 3.32 and 8.79. These diagnostic inheritance factors differ from project-specific design factors. They indicate that groundwater calculations using a single interpreted $T$ and $S$ can become overconfident if the aquifer-test report does not state the interpretation model, residual structure, parameter spread, benchmark support, and field-to-benchmark applicability.

\subsection{Implications and limitations}

The computational benchmark changes the role of field pumping tests in uncertainty analysis. Reported transmissivity and storage are treated as products of an interpretation step, so the workflow records the transformation that produced them. This separates response residuals, parameter model factors, and inherited decision factors. The distinction complements stochastic hydrogeology and groundwater BMA because later models can only propagate uncertainty that is represented in their inputs.

The evidence also defines the applicable domain. The field COV values apply to the two data sets and to the four implemented interpretations. Benchmark model factors are conditional on the scenario class, support definition, observation geometry, pumping schedule, grid design, and screening criteria. Direct evaluation of true field parameter recovery would require independent support-scale measurements. Broader model families remain future extensions, including wellbore storage, skin, delayed yield, generalized radial flow, dual porosity, and unconfined numerical benchmarks. Within that domain, the present result is a reproducible reporting standard: aquifer-test parameters should be accompanied by response model factors, complexity support, pathway-dependent parameter spread, benchmark applicability, and decision inheritance before they are used as deterministic groundwater model inputs.

\section{Conclusions}

This study presents a CAGEO-oriented computational benchmark for aquifer-test transformation uncertainty. The workflow links measured drawdown, four analytical interpretation pathways, response-level log model factors, support-matched numerical reference values, benchmark model-factor distributions, field applicability criteria, and model-averaged decision diagnostics. The 10,000 scenario benchmark produced 160,012 fitted interpretation records; analytical and numerical checks confirmed 22 of 25 analytical limiting cases and 30 of 32 numerical consistency configurations. The results separate response fit, parameter recovery, and decision inheritance.

The two field cases demonstrate the use of the benchmark. Lagging Darcy interpretations reduce response residual structure in both Massachusetts and Lovelock Valley, but leakage support, parameter convergence, and field transfer differ by case. The benchmark-transfer analysis gives conditional model-factor COV values rather than universal design values. The management diagnostic shows that collapsing interpretation uncertainty into one fitted parameter set can understate hydraulic capacity and response-time spread. The main contribution is a reproducible code-backed workflow for reporting how aquifer-test drawdown becomes groundwater model input.
